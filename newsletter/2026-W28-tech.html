Gurugram techie stabs girlfriend, then dies by suicide under train

A technology professional in Gurugram stabbed his girlfriend to death and subsequently died after jumping in front of a train, in a shocking incident reported by India Today. The crime and the suspect's death occurred within hours of each other, leaving investigators to piece together a motive from circumstantial evidence. The case has drawn attention to mental health, domestic violence and the pressures faced by young professionals in India's high-stress tech industry.

→ This tragedy puts a spotlight on domestic violence and mental health crises among India's young urban workforce — issues that affect millions but often go unaddressed until disaster strikes.

23:36 IST

How small-town India is powering the next startup revolution

A new analysis by The Economic Times examines how entrepreneurs and consumers from India's smaller cities and towns are increasingly driving startup activity and innovation, moving beyond the traditional hubs of Bengaluru, Mumbai, and Delhi. The trend reflects improved digital infrastructure, cheaper smartphones, and the proliferation of UPI and vernacular internet, which have combined to make tier-2 and tier-3 India both a fertile ground for new ventures and a massive untapped consumer market. The shift has significant implications for how investors, founders, and policymakers think about India's startup ecosystem.

→ For the hundreds of millions of Indians living outside the country's metros, this trend means the startup economy is finally coming to them — bringing jobs, services, and entrepreneurial opportunity to places previously left behind by the first wave of digital growth.

20:09 IST

India bets ₹1.27 lakh crore on Semicon Mission 2.0 to break China's grip on chip manufacturing

The Union Cabinet has approved ₹1.27 lakh crore for India's Semicon Mission 2.0, a massive push to build domestic semiconductor manufacturing and chip design capabilities. The move is aimed squarely at reducing India's dependence on Chinese supply chains for smartphone and electronics components. It represents one of the largest single technology-sector commitments in Indian government history, and positions India as a serious contender in the global chip race.

→ Semiconductors are inside every smartphone, car, and appliance Indians use — this ₹1.27 lakh crore bet determines whether those chips are ever made in India.

16:04 IST

PE Investments in India Hit $8.7 Billion in First Half of 2026

Private equity investments in India reached $8.7 billion in the first half of 2026, according to Business Standard, signalling continued strong appetite from global and domestic funds for Indian assets. The figure reflects sustained investor confidence in India's growth story even as global markets face uncertainty. PE flows are a key indicator of long-term institutional conviction in an economy.

→ Strong PE inflows mean more capital flowing into Indian businesses — fuelling jobs, startups, infrastructure and new technology — making this a direct indicator of economic health for ordinary Indians.

15:12 IST

No Magnets, No Problem: Indian Startup Takes on China's Rare Earth Monopoly With Software

An Indian startup is challenging China's dominance over rare earth materials — a critical bottleneck in global electric motors and electronics — by using software to eliminate the need for permanent magnets altogether. Rather than sourcing rare earth minerals that China controls, the company has engineered a software-driven approach to motor design that sidesteps the dependency entirely. The development marks a rare homegrown attempt at a geopolitically significant technology problem.

→ China controls the supply of rare earth minerals essential for EVs, defence equipment and electronics — an Indian software fix for this dependency could have enormous strategic and economic consequences for India's manufacturing ambitions.

15:36 IST

India's Startup Founders Are Changing — and So Is the Way They Build Companies

India's startup ecosystem is undergoing a quiet but significant shift in who is founding companies and how they approach building them, according to Livemint. The classic archetype — a young engineer from an IIT or IIM launching a consumer app — is giving way to more diverse founder profiles, including domain experts, ex-corporate executives and second-time entrepreneurs working on deeper thesis-driven bets. The change reflects the maturation of India's startup ecosystem after more than a decade of growth.

→ As India aims to become a global startup hub, the shift toward more experienced, thesis-driven founders could mean fewer flashy apps and more durable, high-impact companies — with real consequences for which problems get solved and which don't.
